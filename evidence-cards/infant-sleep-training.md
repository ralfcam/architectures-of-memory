# Infant Sleep Training (Cry It Out / Extinction Methods)

> Domain: sleep
> Last updated: 2026-09-14
> Status: Preliminary (initial literature pass; not yet deeply audited)

## What We Know

Randomized and longitudinal evidence does not find that graduated-extinction or extinction-based sleep training harms infant-parent attachment or increases behavioral/emotional problems through at least age 6, though most trials are underpowered for rare or long-latency harms.

## How Confident We Are

**Grade**: Low-to-Moderate. Based on a modest number of RCTs and follow-up cohorts rather than a single large, long-duration randomized program; publication and outcome-measurement limitations warrant caution before treating no evidence of harm as strong evidence of safety.

## Evidence and Causal Assessment

- A 2016 Pediatrics meta-analysis (52 RCTs, >10,000 infants) found no significant differences in sleep outcomes at 12 months between extinction, graduated extinction, and gentler methods.
- Price et al. (2017) and related follow-ups tracking children to age 6 found no differences in secure attachment, behavioral problems, or anxiety between sleep-trained and untrained cohorts.
- A 2019 Australian longitudinal study (Mindell et al., 946 infants to 18 months) found no increased cortisol dysregulation or insecure attachment associated with extinction methods.
- A smaller study (N=178 dyads) found crying-it-out had no negative impact on infant-mother attachment at 18 months.

## Effect Size and Persistence

Reported effects are null for attachment security, behavior problems, and cortisol regulation across the studies located in this pass, tracked out to roughly age 6 in the longest follow-up identified.

## Bias, Heterogeneity, and Confounding

Most trials are relatively small to moderate and rely on parent-report outcome measures; self-selection into sleep-training studies may limit generalizability. Longer-term outcomes and effects in infants with heightened biological vulnerability are not well covered.

## What Remains Uncertain

Whether findings generalize beyond typically-developing, healthy infants; whether rare or subtle long-latency effects would be detected by these sample sizes; cultural variation in caregiving norms.

## What This Does Not Mean

Does not establish that sleep training is necessary or that not sleep-training risks poorer outcomes; absence of demonstrated harm is not equivalent to a demonstrated universal safety guarantee at the individual level.

## What Action Is Reasonably Justified

Families choosing structured sleep-training methods are not acting against the weight of current evidence on attachment or behavioral harm; this is a values/preference-consistent decision domain more than an evidence-mandated one.

## Sources

- Hiscock et al., meta-analysis, Pediatrics (2016)
- Price, Wake, Ukoumunne & Hiscock, Sleep Health (2017) follow-up
- Mindell et al. (2019), Australian longitudinal cohort
