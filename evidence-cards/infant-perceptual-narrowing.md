# Infant Perceptual Narrowing for Speech Contrasts

> Domain: language
> Last updated: 2026-09-15
> Status: Source-audited first pass (CH07-C03)

## What We Know

Young infants can discriminate phonetic contrasts they have not yet needed for a native language. Across the first year, that broad sensitivity is reorganized by experience: native-language contrasts remain discriminable, while some nonnative contrasts become harder. This is attunement to the languages actually heard, not a deletion of a universal dictionary.

## How Confident We Are

**Grade**: High that language-specific perceptual reorganization occurs in the first year in the classic Hindi/Salish contrast experiments. **Moderate** for a universal timetable across languages, samples, and methods. **Low** that the change is a permanent closing of later language learning.

## Evidence and Causal Assessment

Werker & Tees (1984) compared English infants with English and Salish adults on a Salish (non-English) contrast, and combined cross-sectional with longitudinal infant data. Infants discriminated the nonnative contrast; the decline appeared within the first year and depended on specific language experience. Identification is experimental (manipulated contrast × age/experience), not a randomized life-course trial. A 2002 reprint of the same paper remains the field’s landmark; later work has questioned how far the original samples generalize.

## Effect Size and Persistence

The outcome is contemporaneous discrimination, not later vocabulary. Persistence of the *decline* into adulthood is well known for some contrasts; recovery and bilingual maintenance show that the system is not sealed.

## Bias, Heterogeneity, and Confounding

Historically narrow language samples; looking-time methods; infant state. Diversity of languages and communities remains a live critique of treating 6–12 months as a single clock.

## What Remains Uncertain

How audiovisual and distributional cues jointly drive the shift; how bilingual input changes the timetable.

## What This Does Not Mean

Does not mean a rigid deadline after which a second language cannot be learned, or that hearing more than one language confuses the infant. Prenatal preference (DeCasper) is a different claim.

## What Action Is Reasonably Justified

Ordinary participation in a linguistic community is the relevant exposure. Phonetic-discrimination training as cognitive enhancement is not justified by this card.
