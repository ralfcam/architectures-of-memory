# **Boundaries, Behavior, and the First Rules**

*Revised Draft*

&nbsp;

*The developmental task of early childhood is not to eliminate the self, but to learn how the self can act with other people.*

# **The Child Who Says No**

A toddler hears a clear instruction: Do not touch the vase. The child looks toward the adult, hears the prohibition, perhaps even repeats it—“No, no”—and then reaches for the vase anyway. To an adult, the sequence can look almost theatrical. The child seems to have heard the rule and chosen to violate it. It is easy to experience the moment as a test of authority: the parent has spoken; the child has refused.

Developmental science suggests a less moralized interpretation. Understanding a request and being able to inhibit an already activated action are related but different capacities. During the first three years, children become increasingly able to delay, restrain, shift, and reorganize behavior, but their performance remains highly dependent on context, attention, language, arousal, fatigue, motivation, and the structure of the situation. Early inhibitory control is observable before age three, yet it is neither unitary nor adult-like, and the same child may succeed in one setting and fail in another. (Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

This distinction matters because ordinary childhood behavior is often described in moral language before the child has acquired the capacities that moral language presupposes. A child who cannot consistently stop an impulse is called stubborn. A child who resists an adult request is called defiant. A child who hits during frustration is called aggressive. A child who complies immediately is called good. These labels collapse developmental processes into character judgments.

The better question is not whether toddlers are obedient. It is how action becomes increasingly coordinated with goals, language, relationships, and recurring social conditions. The transition is gradual and transactional: attention, inhibition, emotion regulation, language, learning, caregiver responses, routines, material conditions, and the child’s own characteristics continually alter one another. No single factor—neurodevelopment, parenting style, punishment, warmth, temperament, or routine—adequately explains it. (Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

# **From Emotion to Action**

Chapter 9 examined the developing organization of emotion. Chapter 10 takes the next step. It asks how a child moves from being helped to settle, wait, recover, and orient toward other people to increasingly organizing what the body does.

Behavioral regulation is not simply emotion regulation with muscles attached. A child may be calm and still be unable to remember a rule, shift attention, inhibit a reach, or select a socially acceptable action. Conversely, a highly aroused child may temporarily lose behavioral control even when the rule is well learned. The systems overlap, but they are not identical.

By the end of the third year, children can sometimes suppress a prepotent response, shift attention, delay an action, and use simple strategies to pursue a goal. Yet these abilities are fragile. Laboratory measures of inhibition in children under three are particularly sensitive to language, motor demands, motivation, familiarity with the task, fatigue, and the meaning of the situation. A failed inhibition task therefore tells us much less about a child’s character than adults often assume. (Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

The developmental sequence is better understood as a changing balance between what the child can organize internally and what the environment still organizes externally. The young child increasingly carries rules in memory, but the room, the routine, the caregiver, the wording of the request, and the emotional climate continue to do part of the regulatory work.

# **The Growing Brake**

Effortful control is one useful way of describing the emerging capacity to deliberately direct attention and inhibit a dominant response in favor of another. Mary Rothbart’s temperament framework treats this capacity as one dimension of individual development rather than as a simple measure of obedience. Behavioral inhibition, by contrast, describes a different phenomenon: wariness or withdrawal in response to novelty or uncertainty. A cautious toddler is not necessarily a well-regulated toddler, and an impulsive toddler is not necessarily incapable of self-control. These processes can coexist in different combinations. (Kochanska; Rothbart; Kagan; Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

The neuroscience is important, but mainly as a description of constraint. Executive systems supporting attention, working memory, monitoring, and inhibition mature over a long period. Their development allows increasingly flexible coordination of behavior, but neural maturation is not a direct readout of a psychological ability, and it does not identify a particular parenting practice as the cause of developmental change.

This is why a toddler can appear astonishingly capable in one moment and profoundly disorganized in the next. The underlying capacity is emerging, not absent; access to the capacity depends on state and context. Fatigue, frustration, hunger, novelty, sensory overload, competing rewards, and rapid demands can all increase the load on systems that are still developing. The child’s apparent inconsistency is therefore part of the developmental phenomenon.

One of the book’s recurring themes reappears here: competence and performance are not the same thing. A child may understand a rule and still fail to act on it. A child may perform a behavior when an adult is nearby but not when the adult is absent. And a child may show excellent control in a simple task while struggling in a richer everyday situation. Development does not proceed by switching capacities on; it proceeds by making them increasingly available across contexts.

## **Why “No” Is Not Yet a Reliable Brake**

Language changes behavioral regulation because a rule can eventually exist in symbolic form before the corresponding action occurs. Research associated with Alexander Luria’s work on verbal regulation showed that the meaning of an instruction develops into a more effective means of guiding action over time. Early in this progression, however, speech itself can be part of the excitation surrounding an action. Later, children increasingly use overt self-directed speech and eventually internalized verbal representations to guide and inhibit behavior. (Luria; Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

The everyday implication is simple but profound: hearing a rule is not the same as having mastered the rule.

Consider the toddler who reaches for the vase after hearing “Don’t touch the vase.” Several operations must be coordinated: attention has to disengage from the attractive object; the prohibition has to be represented; its meaning has to remain active; the child has to suppress an already prepared movement; and an alternative action has to be selected. A breakdown at any point can produce the same outward behavior. The result may look like deliberate disobedience even when the child’s understanding is considerably ahead of their ability to execute the inhibition.

This is why repetition alone is not enough. The child is gradually learning a new kind of behavior: not merely responding to the world, but holding an instruction in mind while acting against an immediate impulse. That achievement belongs to the development of executive control, not to a sudden discovery that adults possess authority.

# **The World Helps the Child Behave**

When internal control is still limited, the environment can carry part of the load. Young children build expectations about recurring events: what happens first, what follows, who usually does what, and what kind of action is likely to be required. Work on event knowledge and generalized event representations describes these recurring scripts as part of how children organize everyday experience.

This gives routines a more useful meaning than the idea that every successful family must operate on a rigid schedule. Predictable sequences may reduce the amount of novelty and decision-making a child must manage. Breakfast that happens in a familiar order, a repeated bedtime sequence, or the same transition from play to leaving the house can make the next action easier to anticipate. The evidence supports routines as promising and plausibly helpful, but much of the literature is observational. Predictability should therefore be treated as a plausible regulatory support, not as a universal causal law. (Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

The distinction matters. A routine is not a prescription for family perfection. It is an environmental support. It can be flexible, culturally shaped, and adjusted to the realities of work, childcare, illness, siblings, disability, housing, and the rhythms of a particular household.

The same principle applies to physical environments. A child who is repeatedly told not to touch a dangerous object is being asked to inhibit the same action over and over. Removing an unnecessary hazard is not permissiveness; it is intelligent allocation of regulatory demands. Child development is partly the art of deciding which demands a developing nervous system needs to practice and which demands adults and environments can reasonably carry for it.

# **Children Learn From What We Do**

Behavior is also learned socially. Bandura’s work established that children can acquire behavior through observation, while later research has shown that imitation depends on attention, relationship, interpretation, incentives, context, and the behavior being modeled. Social referencing adds another layer: when circumstances are ambiguous, young children monitor other people for information about what a situation means and how to respond to it. (Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

This does not mean that children automatically copy everything they see, nor that one parental mistake determines a child’s future. It means that spoken rules exist alongside visible behavior. “Use gentle hands” is one source of information. Watching adults repair conflict without force is another.

The phenomenon of overimitation makes the point especially clearly. In experimental settings, young children have sometimes reproduced actions that are causally unnecessary to achieving a goal. One interpretation is that human children are unusually attentive to the socially demonstrated way of doing things, not only to mechanical efficiency. The evidence is valuable because it shows how children can learn conventions whose purpose is not immediately obvious. It should not, however, be inflated into a claim that observation always overrides instruction or that children simply absorb every behavior without interpretation.

For parents, the practical lesson is less about being a flawless model than about recognizing that social learning is continuous. Children observe how adults handle frustration, how they speak when tired, how they respond to mistakes, how they repair conflict, and how they treat people with less power. The influence of modeling is real without being absolute.

# **When Two Nervous Systems Enter a Struggle**

Behavioral organization can also break down at the level of the interaction itself. Gerald Patterson’s coercion model described how repeated parent-child exchanges can become organized around immediate relief. The child resists; the adult intensifies; the child escalates; the adult eventually withdraws the demand. The child experiences the end of the demand as relief, while the adult experiences the end of the child’s distress as relief. Under some conditions, both sides are inadvertently reinforced by the same sequence.

The important point is not that the parent has “trained” the child to misbehave or that the child has learned to manipulate an adult. It is that behavior is sensitive to consequences, and humans are capable of becoming trapped in mutually reinforcing patterns without consciously choosing them.

A familiar cycle might look like this: a vague request is issued during a transition; the child resists; the adult repeats the request with increasing emotional intensity; the child escalates; the adult gives in simply to end the conflict; everyone experiences temporary relief. The next similar transition now occurs with a history attached to it. The child has learned that escalation sometimes works; the adult has learned that capitulation ends the immediate crisis. Both have been shaped by the interaction.

This is one reason clarity matters. Young children do better when the behavioral demand is understandable, feasible, and proportionate to their developmental capacity. It is also why consistency should not be confused with severity. A calm boundary that an adult can realistically maintain is often more coherent than a dramatic threat that will be abandoned under pressure.

# **Warmth and Structure**

The familiar language of “parenting styles” can be useful historically, but it is too coarse to carry the whole explanation. A more precise description separates dimensions that often travel together: responsiveness, warmth, structure, predictability, and the quality of follow-through. Observational and intervention research suggests that supportive relationships and clear behavioral structure often coexist in helpful environments, while the exact package differs across families and cultures. (Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

Warmth does not mean that every desire is granted. Structure does not mean that every refusal is defeated. The developmental problem is how to provide enough external organization for a child to participate without turning the relationship into an adversarial contest.

This is where Chapter 9’s regulatory relationship becomes directly relevant. A child who is overwhelmed cannot use a boundary simply because the boundary is theoretically correct. The adult is still part of the regulatory environment. Tone, timing, proximity, clarity, and the possibility of repair influence whether the child can use the structure being offered.

The evidence does not establish that one universal parenting style causes good development, nor does it justify treating families as if they were laboratory systems. Culture, temperament, disability, caregiver wellbeing, material resources, childcare arrangements, and the child’s own behavior all shape what structure and responsiveness look like in practice. (Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

# **The First Rules and the Beginning of Internalization**

One of the most important transitions in toddlerhood is from compliance under observation toward increasingly voluntary cooperation. Grazyna Kochanska’s work distinguishes situational forms of compliance from more committed forms in which children more readily align their behavior with an adult’s request. The distinction is valuable precisely because “the child did what I said” does not tell us why the child did it.

A child may comply because an adult is watching, because a threat is present, because the alternative is immediately unpleasant, because the child wants to preserve the interaction, or because the child is beginning to see the rule as meaningful. These are different developmental processes even when the observable behavior is identical.

This makes early conscience a relational rather than purely punitive story. Repeated cooperation, mutual responsiveness, explanation, and opportunities to participate can provide a context in which rules become intelligible and increasingly self-guided. Yet the evidence does not establish a simple pipeline from “good parenting” to conscience. Child characteristics and context remain active parts of the process.

The same caution applies to morality. Young children show early sensitivity to harm, fairness, and social expectations, and research on moral and conventional domains suggests that these kinds of rules are not treated as identical. But toddlers are not miniature moral philosophers. Early sensitivity should be described as the beginning of differentiation, not as a finished moral system.

## **Opposition and the Discovery of a Separate Will**

Noncompliance becomes especially visible when children acquire stronger goals, preferences, language, mobility, and the capacity to resist adult direction. Resistance can express emerging agency, conflict between two valid goals, fatigue, communication difficulty, limited inhibition, or a coercive interaction pattern. The same outward “no” can therefore have different functions. (Kuczynski and colleagues; Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

The developmental significance of opposition is easy to miss because the adult experiences it as friction. But a child who can resist is also a child who can later negotiate, disagree, protect a boundary, refuse an unsafe request, and participate as a separate person. The developmental task is not to eliminate opposition. It is to help the child learn when and how resistance can be expressed, negotiated, revised, or accepted.

This does not make every refusal desirable. Safety-critical limits, hitting, running into danger, and harming others require adult intervention. The distinction is that intervention need not be framed as a verdict on the child’s character. A boundary can stop an action without declaring the child bad for having wanted to perform it.

# **Why Toddlers Hit**

Physical aggression is one of the clearest examples of the difference between development and morality. Longitudinal work has repeatedly found that physical aggression is common in early childhood and generally declines for most children. The peak is typically around the second and third years, followed by decline as language, regulation, and social experience expand. A smaller group shows more persistent elevated aggression, which makes frequency, severity, duration, and trajectory more informative than the mere presence of hitting. (Tremblay and colleagues; Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

The strongest interpretation is not that toddlers “learn violence” from nowhere, nor that aggression is a fixed evolutionary destiny. Rather, physical force is one available behavioral response when children are frustrated, competing, impulsive, aroused, or unable to communicate and inhibit effectively. Development progressively adds alternatives: words, gestures, waiting, seeking help, moving away, negotiating, and remembering what happened before.

Language may therefore contribute to the decline in physical aggression, but language is not a single-cause explanation. Effortful control, temperament, family interaction, opportunity to practice social skills, stress, sleep, neurodevelopmental differences, and environmental demands all matter.

The reassuring conclusion is also the scientifically responsible one: hitting at two is not a forecast of violence at sixteen. Early aggression deserves attention when it is unusually frequent, severe, destructive, persistent, or impairing, but early frequency alone is not destiny. (Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

# **The Child Who Begins to Help**

A chapter about behavioral organization should not describe only what children must stop doing. Young children also begin doing things for other people. Experimental work by Warneken, Tomasello, and colleagues has documented instrumental helping in the second year of life. These behaviors matter because they reveal that social development is not simply the suppression of selfish impulses; it also includes the emergence of capacities for cooperation and attention to another person’s goal.

The interpretation should remain proportionate. Early helping is not identical to mature altruism, empathy, or moral character. Children can help for different reasons, and motivation changes with context. Research on rewards suggests that tangible rewards can sometimes reduce subsequent helping in particular experimental conditions, but that finding should not be expanded into a universal rule that all rewards corrupt prosocial motivation.

The larger developmental point is more durable: children learn what participation feels like. They experience that another person has a goal, that their own action can contribute to it, and that social activity can be organized around more than immediate individual desire. Cooperation is therefore one of the positive forms taken by the developing ability to coordinate action.

# **What Physical Punishment Can—and Cannot—Tell Us**

Physical punishment deserves a careful place in the chapter because the subject exposes the difference between scientific caution and false balance. The observational literature contains real methodological problems. Discipline is not randomly assigned; children’s behavior can evoke parental responses; severity is measured inconsistently; and family stress can influence both punishment and outcomes. Those limitations are reasons to avoid exaggerated causal claims from a single association.

They are not, however, evidence that physical punishment is a necessary developmental technology. The research dossier’s proportionate conclusion is that physical punishment should not be presented as necessary or evidence-based, while the evidence does not support claims that every parent who has ever used it has permanently damaged a child. The appropriate scientific position is neither absolutist rhetoric nor a false equivalence between disputed effect sizes and developmental necessity. (Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

For the purposes of this book, the practical question is therefore not “Does spanking sometimes produce immediate compliance?” It can. The more important question is whether a parent needs physical force to create behavioral organization. The available evidence does not justify that conclusion. Behavioral boundaries can be maintained through non-violent approaches, and coercive physical methods introduce risks without being required for ordinary developmental learning.

This conclusion also keeps responsibility proportional. Institutions should not merely tell stressed families what not to do. Where behavior is dangerous or highly disruptive, practical support, coaching, mental-health care, childcare support, and material assistance may be part of the intervention. Behavioral development is never only a story about parental effort.

# **Culture Changes the Meaning of Behavior**

The meaning of cooperation, respect, autonomy, obedience, and acceptable expression differs across cultures. Developmental research that treats one middle-class Western model as universal risks confusing cultural difference with developmental deficit. The dossier therefore treats culture as part of the causal environment, not as a decorative footnote.

This does not mean that all practices are equally benign. Cultural normativeness can modify how a child experiences and responds to a practice, but it does not turn severe or abusive treatment into a developmental advantage. The proper analytic question is what a practice means in a given social setting, what dose and severity are involved, what alternatives exist, and what outcomes follow over time.

The same principle should govern advice. “Children should respect adults” can carry different meanings across families and cultures. “The child must learn to comply immediately” is a much narrower proposition. The first can express a value about reciprocity and community; the second can turn developmental immaturity into a contest of power.

# **What Actually Helps**

When behavior is persistently difficult, parent-training interventions provide stronger causal evidence than generic claims about parenting style. Programs such as Parent-Child Interaction Therapy, The Incredible Years, and Triple P have been studied as structured approaches to behavioral problems. The research base indicates that such interventions can improve outcomes, especially for proximal parent behavior and parent-reported child difficulties, while effects are generally less uniform for independent observations and longer-term follow-up. Implementation, selection, and informant bias matter. (Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

The lesson is not that every family needs a program. It is that behavioral support can work, and that coaching is often more evidence-based than moral instruction. When a child’s behavior is dangerous, persistent, or severely impairing family life, help should be framed as skill-building and support rather than as proof that the parents have failed.

The same principle applies to prevention. There is more evidence for targeted treatment and indicated intervention than for universal programs producing large durable behavioral transformations in all children under three. The book should therefore resist turning every developmental observation into a new optimization regime.

# **Behavior Is Bigger Than Parenting**

By this point the central transactional principle should be explicit. Children affect adults as adults affect children. A child’s sleep, temperament, sensory profile, language, developmental differences, and behavior can change caregiver stress and responses. Caregiver behavior can alter the child’s immediate experience. Poverty, housing instability, employment, mental health, childcare quality, discrimination, safety, and access to services shape both sides of the transaction. (Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

This matters ethically as much as scientifically. A developmental model that explains everything through parenting turns social conditions into private blame. A family living with chronic housing insecurity does not have the same regulatory environment as a family with predictable space, paid leave, flexible work, and abundant childcare. A child with unusual sensory or communication demands does not present the same behavioral task as another child. The relevant unit is often the developmental system, not the isolated parent.

That does not make parents powerless. It clarifies what power means. Parents can shape routines, language, opportunities, boundaries, and interactions. They can also seek help, redesign environments, and repair mistakes. But they operate inside systems that constrain what is possible. Scientific interpretation becomes more useful when it makes both influence and constraint visible.

# **A Developmental Map of Behavioral Organization**

The chapter can now be summarized as a set of interacting developmental pathways rather than a single causal pipeline.

First, the regulatory pathway: the child’s ability to act depends partly on whether arousal remains within a state in which attention and inhibition can be recruited. Emotional regulation therefore creates conditions for behavioral organization without reducing behavior to emotion.

Second, the representational pathway: language and memory allow rules, goals, and alternative actions to exist in symbolic form. A child can increasingly remember what should happen before the action is performed.

Third, the social-learning pathway: children observe, imitate, receive feedback, monitor other people, and participate in recurring routines. The social world supplies models and meanings, not merely rewards and punishments.

Fourth, the environmental pathway: predictable sequences and sensible physical arrangements can reduce unnecessary regulatory load. Environment and behavior continually shape one another.

Fifth, the transactional pathway: repeated interactions can scaffold learning or create coercive cycles. The child’s behavior modifies the adult’s response; the adult’s response modifies the child’s next behavior.

None of these should be treated as an independently measured explanation of all behavioral development. They are components of an interacting system. The value of the model is not that it produces a simple formula. It is that it explains why the same behavior can arise from different causes, why the same child behaves differently in different contexts, and why developmental change cannot be reduced to parental technique.

# **The Social Organization of Agency**

The deepest mistake in thinking about early behavior is to begin from the assumption that the child’s primary problem is insufficient obedience. That framing places adult authority at the center of a developmental process in which the child is actually becoming an agent.

The toddler wants something. The toddler notices that another person wants something else. The toddler discovers that some actions produce predictable consequences. The toddler learns words for requests, prohibitions, emotions, and plans. The toddler encounters limits. The toddler resists them. The toddler discovers that resistance does not always succeed and that cooperation sometimes produces a better outcome. The toddler begins to remember rules when no adult is watching. The toddler begins to help someone else pursue a goal.

This is not a story of the self being broken into obedience. It is a story of the self acquiring structure.

Boundaries are part of that structure because agency without any coordination cannot become participation. But boundaries work developmentally when they are embedded in a relationship and an environment the child can use. They need to be clear enough to guide action, stable enough to be predictable, proportionate enough to be feasible, and flexible enough to accommodate developmental variation.

The child therefore does not graduate from dependence into independence in a single leap. External regulation becomes increasingly internal organization, but the external environment never disappears. Adults continue to create the conditions under which self-control is easier or harder to exercise. Even adults depend on routines, institutions, reminders, social expectations, and other people. What changes during early childhood is the balance between regulation supplied by others and regulation increasingly carried by the child.

# **Science, Interpretation, and Art**

Science gives us a cautious but coherent picture. Behavioral organization develops over infancy and toddlerhood. Inhibitory control becomes more measurable and more flexible, while remaining context-sensitive. Language, memory, emotion regulation, social learning, relationships, routines, and material conditions interact. Physical aggression is common and generally declines for most children. Prosocial behavior appears early. Targeted behavioral interventions can help, while long-term and universal effects are less certain. Physical punishment is not established as a necessary developmental tool. (Chapter 10 Research Dossier)

Interpretation takes the next step, but must remain visibly interpretive. Rules matter because relationships and institutions give rules meaning, feasibility, and emotional consequences. Predictability can function as an external support while internal regulation develops. Repeated cooperation can make social expectations increasingly intelligible and self-directed. None of these statements is a single experimentally isolated mechanism.

Art begins where measurement stops. A parent watches a two-year-old say “no” and can experience both exhaustion and wonder. The child is difficult because the child has become capable of having a different intention. The same refusal that creates conflict is evidence that another person is emerging there: a mind with preferences, memory, purposes, and a will that is not identical to the adult’s.

The first rules are therefore not the end of freedom. They are among the conditions from which a new kind of freedom can eventually emerge—the capacity to have an impulse without being ruled by it, to have a desire without making it another person’s obligation, to disagree without destroying the relationship, and to act with other people rather than merely alongside them.

# **Closing: The First Rules**

*A young child does not become socially capable by learning to suppress the self. The child becomes socially capable by learning, with other people, how to organize the self.*

By three years of age, the child can do more than the infant could: wait sometimes, stop sometimes, remember sometimes, cooperate sometimes, help sometimes, and carry simple expectations from one situation into another. “Sometimes” is the important word. Development has not finished. The child still depends on adults, environments, language, routines, and relationships. But a new capacity is becoming visible.

The adult’s task is therefore not to win every contest. It is to help create conditions in which the child can gradually carry more of the organization themselves. That means protecting safety, setting boundaries, designing environments that do not impose unnecessary demands, modeling the behaviors we hope children will acquire, and treating mistakes as opportunities for learning and repair.

The first rules do not mark the moment when childhood becomes obedient. They mark the beginning of a deeper developmental achievement: the child is learning that their actions exist inside a world of other minds, other bodies, shared purposes, competing goals, and reciprocal obligations.

The story of behavior is thus the story of agency becoming social.
